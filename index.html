Job Market Papers

Employer Competition and Certification

Abstract JMP Click to expand
This paper develops a theory of employer competition over hiring standards in labor markets where employers rely on third-party certification to screen applicants. The certifier sells tests to an applicant, who possesses noisy private information about his ability and seeks to persuade employers to offer him employment. An employer hires if, conditional on the test outcome, her posterior belief about the applicant’s ability meets her standard.

Employers compete in their selectivity or hiring standards to attract the applicant. The certifier faces both a screening problem (due to applicants’ private information) and an information-design problem (tests must be informative enough to persuade employers). These screening frictions affect the equilibrium determination of hiring standards and the certification mechanism.

Relative to a benchmark with only information design, I show two distortions. First, unemployment rises: the certifier’s optimal selling mechanism excludes some applicant types. Second, competition among employers intensifies: inefficient test allocation steers applicants toward less selective employers, leading to an equilibrium constriction of standards.

Working Papers

Certifying Lemons with Discernible Hard Information (Email me for a draft); Slides for an older version

Abstract Click to expand
A sender with private information (high or low ability) tries to convince a receiver of having higher ability. A certifier offers a menu of Blackwell experiments and prices to screen the sender. An experiment produces information conditional on the sender’s ability, and the sender uses the outcome to persuade the receiver to take a favorable action.

I characterize equilibrium interactions when the receiver can distinguish outcomes via their hard information (likelihood ratios). I prove a decomposition result: every equilibrium outcome is a convex combination of menus containing only simple experiments (outcomes supported on sets of size at most three). Using this, I show the existence of an equilibrium in which soft information overrules hard information—due to equilibrium self-selection, some outcomes whose hard information is not sufficient to persuade the receiver nonetheless end up persuading the receiver.
